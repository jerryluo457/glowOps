Replication study · Conflict Management and Peace Science 28(2), 2011

Unpacking Escalation, replicated

Braithwaite, Alex, and Douglas Lemke. 2011. "Unpacking Escalation." Conflict Management and Peace Science 28(2): 111–123. doi:10.1177/0738894210396631

The article's central claim replicates. Across six ways of measuring escalation, a territorial dispute is the only covariate that consistently makes a militarized dispute more likely to escalate. It is significant in five of six models and insignificant only for Use of Force, as published. That pattern holds in all nine specifications estimated here, including one that corrects two coding errors found in the original data.

Two coding errors in the original data

Stata treats a missing value (.) as larger than any number. Two of the article's variables behave exactly as if missing codes had been converted to . and then compared with > or >=.

Missing fatality levels counted as escalation

MID records fatalities as a band: code 4 is 251–500 deaths, 6 is more than 999, and −9 is missing. All 2,331 disputes in MID 3.0 contain only 104 with more than 250 deaths, yet the article counts 294 among its 1,901 bilateral disputes.

Polity special codes counted as democratic

The article names eight "jointly democratic" warring dyads. Four of them include a state whose Polity IV score is a special code: −66 (foreign interruption) or −88 (transition).

What survives the corrections

Holds

Territorial disputes escalate. The effect is unchanged for four measures and stronger for Fatalities > 250 (0.25 → 0.58). Use of Force remains the single exception.

Holds

Joint democracy raises the risk of mutual use of force. 0.24** with corrected coding, against 0.25* published.

Does not hold

Joint democracy raises the risk of war. Published at 0.36**. Corrected, the coefficient is −0.05 (p ≈ 0.82). The published result rests on dyads miscoded as democratic.

Does not hold

Joint satisfaction lowers the risk of Fatalities > 250. Published at −0.37**. Corrected, the coefficient is +0.08 and insignificant.

Specification

Rebuilt from the article's text, using data versions current in 2011 wherever possible.

Unit and sample
Non-directed dyad-years, 1919–1995. 500,225 observations (published: 499,145).
Stage 1: MID onset
A bilateral MID begins in the dyad-year, from COW MID v3.02. "Bilateral on day one" means exactly one originator per side. 1,457 onsets (published: 1,352).
Stage 2: six measures
Reciprocated (recip), Use of Force (hostlev ≥ 4), Mutual Use of Force (each side reaches hostlev ≥ 4), Fatalities > 0, Fatalities > 250, and War (hostlev = 5).
Both stages
Joint democracy (Polity IV democ ≥ 6), joint satisfaction (positive alliance-portfolio τ-b with the UK to 1945 and the US after), power preponderance (stronger state's CINC share), and defense pact (COW Alliances v3.03).
Territory
Stage 1 uses Huth & Allee territorial claims. Stage 2 uses MID revision type 1 for either originator.
Stage 1 only
Contiguity (land or ≤ 150 miles of water), strategic rivalry, and both-minor-powers. Peace years enter as a cubic polynomial.
Estimator
Censored (Heckman) probit by maximum likelihood with dyad-clustered sandwich errors, written in Python and validated on simulated data.

Limitations

  • Stage 2 has 7.8% more disputes than published. No definition of "bilateral on day one" reproduces the article's 1,901 disputes or its 104 wars; the escalation shares still match within two percentage points.
  • Table 2 reproduces only in part. The baseline probabilities match (e.g., 0.508 vs. 0.48 for Reciprocated), but replicated territorial effects are about 1.5–2 times larger than published, even though the coefficients agree.
  • Rivalry uses the 2021 update of Thompson's strategic rivalries rather than the 2001 list. τ-b is computed here rather than taken from EUGene.
  • The article defines contiguity as "fewer than 125km of water," a threshold COW does not code. Alternative definitions are run as robustness checks and change nothing substantive.
  • The estimator was not cross-checked against Stata or R. It recovers known parameters on simulated data (|z| < 1.5).
